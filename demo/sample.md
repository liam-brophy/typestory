# The Margin Keeper

## Chapter One | The Room Above the Bindery

The bindery closed at six, but the room above it kept its own hours. Wren had rented it for the north light and the smell of glue, which she found calming in a way she could not explain to anyone who had not grown up around presses. The floorboards were scored with the ghosts of old machinery, long rectangles where iron feet had stood for a century, and on humid evenings the whole building seemed to remember what it had once been for.

She worked at a long table under the window. On it, in careful stacks, were the proofs of a book that did not yet exist: four hundred pages of a novel by a writer who had died before finishing it, assembled by an estate that wanted it beautiful and wanted it soon. Wren had been hired to set it. That was the word they used, *set*, as if type were a bone and she were the one who would hold it still while it healed.

The first thing she noticed was the margin. Not the text, which was dense and good and occasionally strange, but the space beside it. Someone had been drawing there. Small figures, in a brown ink gone soft with age, ran down the outer edge of the manuscript pages: a cube balanced on one corner, a seed head bursting into rays, a long animal with too many legs that seemed to be walking out of the page entirely.

None of the drawings were mentioned in the estate's letter. None of them, as far as she could tell, had anything to do with the story. And yet they had been made with the patience of someone who expected them to be kept.

## Chapter Two | A Grammar of Space

There is a rule every compositor learns early and spends the rest of her life testing: the white of the page is not empty. It is a material, like the ink, and it can be spent well or badly. A paragraph set too wide becomes a field the eye cannot cross without losing its place. Set too narrow, it stutters, every line a small interruption. The right measure is a kind of breathing.

Wren set the first chapter three times. The first version was correct and lifeless. The second was handsome and slightly too proud of itself, the kind of page that wants to be photographed rather than read. The third she made at night, without looking at the clock, and in the morning she could not remember choosing any of it. That was the one she kept.

It was while proofing the third version that she saw the seed head again. Not in the manuscript this time but in her own pages, in the right margin beside the opening of the second chapter, drawn in the same soft brown ink. She had not put it there. She was certain of that the way one is certain of a locked door: by the memory of the act, and by the absence of any other explanation that would let her sleep.

She touched it, half expecting it to smear. It did not. But when she lifted her finger, the words nearest to it had moved. *Only slightly*, she would tell herself later. A line that had ended on *breathing* now ended on *kind*, and the word *breathing* sat alone at the start of the next line, as though it had stepped aside to let something pass.

## Chapter Three | The Habits of Lines

She began keeping a notebook. In it she recorded each appearance: the date, the page, the figure, and what the text around it did. The cube preferred the openings of chapters and rarely moved. The seed head wandered. The long animal she saw only once, near the end of a chapter about a flood, and when it moved the words did not merely step aside but scattered, like starlings lifting from a field, before settling back into their lines a little further down the page.

What struck her most was that the text always recovered. However far the words were pushed, they returned to their original order and their original shape. The paragraph remembered itself. It was, she wrote, less like a page being damaged and more like a pond after a stone: a disturbance, a widening ring, and then the same water as before.

> A line of type is a promise about where the eye will go next. Break the promise too often and the reader stops trusting you. Break it once, beautifully, and they may follow you anywhere.

She found the passage pencilled inside the cover of an old specimen book on the shelf by the door. It was unsigned. She copied it into her notebook and underlined *once*.

* * *

By the end of the month she had stopped being frightened. The figures did not seem to want anything from her except attention, and attention was the one thing her work had always required. She began, cautiously, to make room for them: a slightly wider outer margin, a little more air at the head of each chapter. She told the estate it was a design decision. It was, in a way.

## Chapter Four | What the Estate Wanted

The estate wanted the drawings removed. Their letter was polite and very clear. The manuscript, they said, was a literary work, and the marginalia were the private habit of a writer who had never intended them to be seen. Wren was to reproduce the text faithfully and nothing else.

She read the letter twice and then went upstairs and sat at the long table until the light went. The proofs lay in their stacks. In the margin of the top sheet, the cube sat on its corner, perfectly still, the way a cat sits when it knows it is being discussed.

The difficulty was that she no longer believed the drawings and the text were separate things. She had watched the words move around them. She had seen how a paragraph changed its breath when the seed head drifted near, how certain sentences seemed to lean toward the margin as though listening. To remove the figures would be to remove something the text had been shaped around, the way a riverbed is shaped by a stone that has since been carried away.

She wrote back to the estate. Her letter was also polite and also very clear. Then she put the kettle on, because whatever happened next was going to take a while.

## Chapter Five | Settling

The book was printed in the spring. It had generous margins and nothing in them. The estate was pleased; the reviews used the word *restrained*, which Wren had learned to hear as praise.

But she had made one other edition, for herself, on the small press downstairs after the bindery closed. Thirty copies, sewn by hand. In those, the margins held every figure she had recorded in her notebook, each one placed beside the passage where it had first appeared. She did not know whether they would move for anyone else. She suspected that was not the point.

On the last night, before she gave up the room, she sat at the long table and read her copy from the beginning. Near the end of the flood chapter the long animal lifted itself out of the margin, walked once across the page, and the words rose around it and fell back into place. When it reached the far edge it stopped, and turned, and looked at her, or seemed to.

Then the lines were still again, exactly as she had set them. The paragraph remembered itself. She closed the book, turned off the light, and listened for a while to the building remembering what it had once been for.
